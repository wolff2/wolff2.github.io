How to Price a Cake: A Simple Formula for Home Bakers

Updated November 10, 2026 · LumivexaStore

Most home bakers start by copying what other people charge. The problem is that other people have different ingredient prices, different recipes and different expectations for their own time. A price that works for them can quietly lose you money. This guide gives you a simple formula you can use for every cake, cupcake order or cookie box.

The cake pricing formula

Price = (Ingredients + Packaging + Decorations + Your time + Overhead) ÷ (1 − Profit margin) + Delivery

Each part matters. Leave one out and the cake that looks profitable is actually costing you.

Step 1: Cost the ingredients for this exact cake

Use the recipe you will actually bake, not a rough guess. For each ingredient, divide the package price by the package size to get a cost per gram, cup or piece, then multiply by the amount the recipe uses. Include the things that are easy to forget: butter for greasing, food colouring, vanilla, sprinkles and the second batch of frosting you always end up making.

Step 2: Add packaging and decorations

Cake boards, boxes, cupcake liners, ribbons, toppers, fresh flowers and labels all cost money. Write them down per order. A $3 box on a $40 cake is more than 7% of the price.

Step 3: Pay yourself for your time

This is the line most home bakers skip. Count every hour: shopping, baking, cooling, decorating, cleaning and answering messages. Pick an hourly rate you would accept for skilled work in your area. Even a modest rate changes the picture: three hours at $15 an hour adds $45 to the cost of a cake.

Step 4: Add a share of overhead

Electricity for the oven, baking tools that wear out, your website or Etsy fees, card payment fees and any licence or permit costs are overhead. A simple method is to add up these costs for a month and divide by the number of orders you usually make.

Step 5: Apply your profit margin the right way

Profit is what lets you replace your mixer, invest in new tins and grow. Many bakers add 20–40% on top of costs, but there is a common mistake here. Adding 25% to your cost (a markup) does not give you a 25% margin. To get a true margin, divide by (1 − margin):

  • Cost $78, markup 25% → $78 × 1.25 = $97.50 (your real margin is only 20%)
  • Cost $78, margin 25% → $78 ÷ 0.75 = $104.00 (you keep 25% of the price)

A worked example

ItemAmount
Ingredients$18.00
Packaging (board + box)$4.00
Decorations$6.00
Your time (3 hours × $15)$45.00
Overhead share$5.00
Total cost$78.00
Price at 25% margin ($78 ÷ 0.75)$104.00
Delivery (charged separately)$10.00
Quote to the customer$114.00

Check your price against the market, last

Only after you know your real cost should you look at local prices. If your number is much higher, look for savings in the recipe or the design, or offer a simpler version at a lower price. If your number is lower than the market, you have room to charge more. Avoid simply matching a lower price: you would be paying for your customer's cake.
