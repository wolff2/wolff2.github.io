How to Split Caregiving Costs Fairly Between Siblings

Updated November 10, 2026 · LumivexaStore

Caring for a parent is expensive, and money is one of the most common reasons siblings fall out. Usually it is not because anyone is unfair. It is because nobody wrote down what was paid, who paid it, and what was agreed. A clear, simple system prevents most of those arguments.

Step 1: Agree what counts as a shared cost

Sit down (or video call) and agree on a list. Common shared costs include:

  • Home care aides, respite care and day programs
  • Medication co-pays and medical supplies
  • Mobility aids and home safety changes, such as grab bars or a ramp
  • Transport to appointments
  • Groceries or meal deliveries, if your parent can no longer cover them

Also agree what is not shared, such as your own fuel for a routine visit or a gift you chose to buy. Writing this down once saves a hundred awkward conversations.

Step 2: Choose a split method

There is no single right answer. Families usually choose one of these:

  • Equal shares. Every sibling pays the same percentage. Simple and easy to explain.
  • Income-based shares. Siblings who earn more pay a larger percentage, for example 50 / 30 / 20.
  • Time-adjusted shares. The sibling who provides most of the hands-on care pays a smaller share of costs, because their time is their contribution.

Whatever you choose, write the percentages down and agree to review them every six months or when circumstances change.

Step 3: Log every cost as it happens

For each expense, record the date, what it was, the amount, who paid, and whether it is shared. Keep receipts in one shared folder. Logging as you go is far easier than reconstructing months of spending later.

Step 4: Calculate who owes whom

Once a month, add up all shared costs. Each person's fair share is the total multiplied by their percentage. Compare that to what each person actually paid:

A positive balance means that person paid more than their share and is owed money; a negative balance means they owe. In this example, Sophie pays Anna about $618 and the family is level again.

Step 5: Keep it visible to everyone

Transparency is what keeps the peace. Use one shared document that every sibling can open at any time, rather than numbers living in one person's head or in a long group chat. When everyone can see the same figures, the conversation shifts from "who is paying more" to "what does Mom need next".

Talk about the hard parts early

Decide in advance what happens if costs rise sharply, if one sibling cannot pay for a while, or if your parent's own savings should be used first. These are easier to agree on calmly than in a crisis.
